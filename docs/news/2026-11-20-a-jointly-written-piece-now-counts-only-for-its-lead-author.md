When one of the 37 people on my expert panel signed a piece written with other people, I used to count its forecast as that person's own words, and count it again for every other panel member who signed it. From November 20th, 2026, a piece written with others counts only for its lead author, the person named first in its byline ([the rule](/docs/amendments)). A piece signed by one person still counts for that person, as before.

The reason is that a forecast in a group piece is the group's wording, and the people named after the first may not have chosen it. Counting it once for each panel member who signed it also weighs one piece several times, so a group's view counts for more than any one person's. The lead author is the one person a reader can most fairly hold to the words. A written dialogue, where each turn is marked with its speaker, is a conversation rather than a jointly written piece, so each person's own turns still count for them, as they would in an interview ([the rule](/docs/amendments)).

The question came up while I was reading [a new essay from Google DeepMind](https://institute.deepmind.com/essays/what-comes-after-agi/) on the paths from AGI to superintelligence. It rests on a report whose byline names [Shane Legg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shane_Legg), a [Google DeepMind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google_DeepMind) co-founder who is on the panel, last of fourteen authors. Under the new rule that report gives Legg no statement, and the same goes for two other recent posts where panel members are named third ([the rule](/docs/amendments)).

Applied to everything already collected, the rule leaves 946 expert statements counted instead of 966, and 361 claims instead of 364 ([every expert statement, as a table](/data/statements.csv); [the claims page](/claims/)). The largest share is 8 statements by [Arvind Narayanan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arvind_Narayanan) from posts where his co-author [Sayash Kapoor](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/~sayashk/) is named first. [Stuart Russell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stuart_J._Russell) and [Max Tegmark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Tegmark) each lose 2 statements from a 2014 opinion piece led by [Stephen Hawking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Hawking), and [Ilya Sutskever](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ilya_Sutskever) loses 3 statements and 3 claims from two [OpenAI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OpenAI) posts led by [Sam Altman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sam_Altman) and [Jan Leike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jan_Leike) ([the rule](/docs/amendments)).

That moves one sentence on the home page, the one that compares the people building AI with the people studying it. The builders have named about 2030 all along, and the people studying AI have come down to about 2034 across all their guesses since 2023 ([the home page](/)). Neither of those changed. What changed is where the people studying AI started: their earliest guesses used to sit at 2100 or later, and now they sit at about 2076, because the far-off guesses in the 2014 opinion piece and in a 2016 report by a study panel were among those earliest guesses, and no panel member led either one ([the rule](/docs/amendments)).

Nothing is deleted. The statements and claims from pieces a person did not lead stay in the downloadable tables, marked, and the full list is at the end of the amendments page under "Kept, not counted" ([the rule](/docs/amendments)). Where a lead author is on the panel, as Altman and Leike are, their own rows still count, so no piece drops out of the record entirely when a panel member led it. Who is on the panel does not change either: changing that needs its own amendment ([the rule](/docs/amendments)).
