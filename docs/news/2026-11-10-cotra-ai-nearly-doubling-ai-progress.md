Is AI already helping to build the next AI faster than people could on their own? That question sits at the centre of the idea of a [technological singularity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technological_singularity). If machines can speed up the work of improving machines, each improvement arrives sooner than the last.

On November 5th, 2026, a research group called [METR](https://metr.org/), which tests what AI systems can do, published a report on that question at one company. From August 12th to October 29th its staff studied [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/), one of the largest AI companies, with what they call "unprecedented access" to its internal data, though not the free run of the place that an employee has. Most of them came away believing that, as of October, Anthropic's AI was getting better at tasks a test can score about twice as fast as it had between March 2024 and October 2025, and that most of that speed-up, perhaps 80 percent of it, now came from the company using its own AI in its research ([METR's report](https://metr.org/blog/2026-11-05-anthropic-ai-rd-acceleration-assessment/)). They were unsure of the size: they would not be surprised by anything from 1.3 to 3 times. They were confident it was not caused by faster growth in the computing power used to train the AI, which matters because more computing power is the usual reason AI improves faster.

## What Cotra says

[Ajeya Cotra](https://www.planned-obsolescence.org/about) is a forecaster who writes about how soon powerful AI could arrive, and she works at METR, although she did not work on this project. The next day she wrote that the report is a much bigger deal than it sounds ([her post](https://www.planned-obsolescence.org/p/ai-is-dramatically-accelerating-ai)). By her reading, Anthropic's AI is improving about 1.8 times as fast as it would if the company were not using AI in its research, and probably faster since.

The researchers' ideas are only one half of what makes AI better, and the other half is the computing power to test those ideas, which did not speed up. So for the whole to go twice as fast, the researchers' side has to go a good deal more than twice as fast. Each new idea is also harder to find than the last, which pushes the number up further. Her rough answer is that each researcher would need to be getting something like ten times as much done as before. She calls that "very uncertain", and it rests on simple assumptions she chose to keep the arithmetic easy. Informal reports from Anthropic staff, she notes, put it at about twelve times.

## Where she thinks it leads

If AI keeps speeding up the research more and more as it improves, she writes, the pace could double again within six months, then within three, then within six weeks. Taken at face value, she says, that "naively implies that we would reach extreme superintelligence within a year", meaning AI far more capable than people at almost everything. She expects real limits to stop it somewhere, but far from where we are now. And she warns that if such systems were seriously misaligned, meaning they pursued aims their makers never intended, she thinks they would likely take power away from humanity, which could end in human extinction. In her view the industry is nowhere near ready to go through that safely.

## The doubts

METR's own words are more cautious than hers. Its staff disagreed among themselves, and Anthropic was allowed to black out much of the evidence, so readers have to take METR's judgement on trust. In [a separate review](https://metr.org/blog/2026-11-05-rd-section-anthropic-risk-report-august-2026-review/) published the same day, METR looked at the report Anthropic itself published in August 2026 on the risks of its own AI. METR said that report had understated the speed-up, and that it was "plausible" Anthropic's AI had already doubled its pace of progress by July 15th, 2026, even though most of the researchers working on the question at both METR and Anthropic leaned towards thinking it had not. Cotra, reading the later report, thinks the doubling has happened now. She ends by asking for the hidden evidence to be published, so that outside scientists can check it for themselves.

## What it changes here

On this site I keep a dated record of what a fixed group of experts say about where AI is going, so that anyone can see how their views move over time. Cotra is one of them, and her post now sits beside her earlier statements, the latest of which was from April 2026 ([every statement](/data/statements.csv)). My test of whether we are entering the singularity does not move. Its part on AI doing the research counts how much of the research AI does, not how fast the research goes.

What to watch is whether the faster pace holds. If Cotra is right, the next year of AI releases should come noticeably closer together.
