# Proposal: Tesla's Optimus robot, and where it fits in the tracker family

**Status: a proposal, written 2026-10-21 by the agent, at John's request of the same day. Nothing
here is entered. No claim file, search-log row, table or page changes until John says yes. The
draft entries below are written so that they can be copied in as they stand.**

John's question (2026-10-21): people are discussing Tesla's earnings call, which reportedly says
Optimus humanoid robots are entering production at its Fremont factory. Where does this kind of
progress fit in the tracker family, or is it a new area worth tracking?

## 1. What Tesla actually said

Tesla reported its results for July to September 2026 (its third quarter) after the market closed
on 2026-10-21 and held its call with investors the same afternoon.

**In writing.** Tesla's results deck, filed with the US Securities and Exchange Commission as
exhibit 99.1 to a report filed 2026-10-21
([the deck on the SEC's site](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1318605/000162828026066738/exhibit9914.htm),
opened 2026-10-21; Tesla's own investor page refused automated reading):

- Page 6, "Manufacturing & Hardware": "Optimus production began in Fremont on our high-volume
  lines. The bots coming off this line are the first production-quality bots that we have
  manufactured." And: "we have proven that the current manufacturing process for Optimus is
  suitable for a commercial product."
- Page 6, the factory table: California, "Optimus - Early Ramp"; Texas, "Optimus - Construction".
- Page 9, "Outlook": "Production has begun for Tesla Semi in Nevada and Optimus in Fremont."
- The deck gives no number of robots built, no rate, no start date, no price, no outside
  customer and no version name.

**On the call.** Tesla's own recording,
["Tesla Q3 2026 Financial Results and Q&A Webcast"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tFezqXBF-gw)
(uploaded 2026-10-21), read through its automatic captions on 2026-10-21. These are caption words,
not a checked transcript. Elon Musk, Tesla's chief executive, unless marked:

- 9:43: "we've started like what I'd call early production on a high volume line in Fremont."
- 10:19 to 10:42: most of a production line meant for very high volume has "landed in Fremont",
  but "it takes us probably at least a year, maybe a little longer ... to get to 20,000 units a
  week".
- 11:33 to 11:53: "there's still some things we need to tweak about the design", and "still
  quite a lot of work to do with regard to the AI and the robot um for it to be truly useful for
  open-ended tasks."
- 12:17 to 12:31: "I do expect these issues to be solved around the end of next year. Um, so
  that that's the time to to expect um when we're delivering Optimus to uh uh to companies
  outside of Tesla." He repeats it at 1:02:27: delivering to customers "is likely to be towards
  the end of next year."
- 12:49 to 12:58: "I don't want to temper expectations" (he means the opposite, from the words
  that follow) "we still have a lot to get through and it's about about a year or so".
- 26:36 to 27:29: "we may be ready to release like an Optimus video um in a month or so", showing
  "Optimus doing things that um that are not pre-programmed", "maybe a month or two".
- 27:34 to 27:51: Tesla's next AI chip, AI5, goes into Optimus "if things go according to plan
  ... in approximately uh April of next year".
- 29:21 to 30:04: the first customers would be industrial: "dangerous repetitive tasks", work
  "you want done basically 24/7"; robots people can buy for the home come later.
- 1:02:35, another executive: "we've also started constructing a factory here in Austin".

**Do the reports overstate it?** Partly. "Production has begun" is Tesla's own claim, so a
headline saying production started is fair. Read as robots coming off the line in numbers, it is
not: Musk called it "early production", gave no count, and said the design is still changing,
especially the hands. Some headlines turn "at least a year, maybe a little longer" into "about a
year", and "I do expect ... around the end of next year" into a firm date for outside customers.
Nothing on the call or in the deck says Optimus is doing useful work in Tesla's factories today;
on the call of 2026-01-28 Musk said it was "not in usage in our factories in a material way"
(already in the ledger's search log, the row for that call, `musk-claims-016`).

### 2b. The singularity test's economy part (part D)

Part D is scored on one number only: how fast world output grows in a year, from the World Bank,
against a line of 30 percent a year (`docs/singularity-entry-definition.md`, leg D). Robots enter
it only through that number. No count of robots, and no company statement, scores it. Changing
that would mean changing the frozen test, which only John can do by amendment.

What would count as measured evidence that robots are doing paid physical work, if John ever
wanted it shown beside part D (shown, not scored, as part C does with company code figures):

- **A count made by someone other than the maker**, of robots doing paid work for a customer
  outside the maker's own company: a trade body's census (the
  [International Federation of Robotics](https://www.ifr.org/worldrobotics/report-2025) counts
  industrial robots installed each year, 542,000 in 2024, but does not yet count humanoids
  separately), a government survey, or a regulator's register.
- **A customer's own published figures**, such as hours worked or tasks done by robots in its
  operations, in an annual report or filing.
- **Not evidence:** a maker saying production has begun, a target rate, a demonstration video, or
  a forecast. Tesla's deck of 2026-10-21 is all of these and none of the first two. It is a
  company announcement, so it would be shown, never scored, and today there is nowhere on the
  test where it belongs.

### 2c. The "What the companies said" table

No. That table sits under part C, which asks whether AI is doing the work of improving AI, and
holds companies' figures for how much of their own AI research or code their AI does. A robot
factory is not AI research. Putting Optimus there would muddle the one thing that table measures.

## 3. The other trackers

- **The Belt Equation:** no. Its steps are about launch, orbit, the Moon and asteroids. The only
  robot wording is in the step about a large structure assembled in orbit
  (`L-large-structure-assembled-in-orbit`), and that step asks for most joins made without a
  spacewalk, by any means. A humanoid robot in a California factory bears on none of them. The
  Mars-launch post above is a rocket date and also matches no step.
- **The Upgrade Tree:** no. It tracks how much of a human being can be changed on purpose. Its
  robotic-hand items are about a person controlling a robot arm through a brain implant, which is
  a change to the person. A separate robot doing a job changes no one.
- **Outrunning Ageing and The Quiet Galaxy:** not touched by this.

## 4. Should there be a new tracker? ("When could a robot do my job, or my chores?")

**What it would measure.** How much paid physical work, and how much housework, machines do
without a person controlling them, measured by someone other than the maker. It would also show
the dated promises against what came.

**What public data exists.** Thin, and mostly not about the question.

- Factory robot counts exist: the International Federation of Robotics publishes yearly totals of
  industrial robots installed. But these are fixed arms doing one task. They don't answer "could a
  robot do my job", and the full report is paid.
- Humanoid and home robots have almost no outside count. What exists is makers' announcements,
  demonstration videos, small pilot projects, and targets. Under the family's rules all of this is
  shown, not scored.
- Driverless cars are the one area with outside counts (regulators' permits and crash reports).
  They are a robot doing a paid physical job, and they are the strongest case for a tracker. But
  they are a large separate field.

**The upkeep.** A weekly scan of makers and customers, plus a yearly read of the trade counts.
Most weeks it would turn up only announcements, which by our own rule cannot move a number. So
the site would be mostly shown material with very little scored, which is the weakness the family
has worked to avoid.

**What the AGI Zeitgeist already covers.** The promises: the ledger records Musk's dated robot
claims, and those of the other leaders, and shows them coming due. It does not cover what robots
actually do. No sibling site does.

**Recommendation: not now.** Record the three claims in the AGI Zeitgeist ledger, which captures
what is newsworthy this week: a dated promise, and a chain of dates that keeps moving. Look at a
robot tracker again when an outside count of humanoid or general-purpose robots doing paid work
exists. The likeliest sources are the International Federation of Robotics counting humanoids on
their own, or Tesla delivering to outside customers (on its own words, around the end of 2027).
If John wants something sooner, the cheaper step is a short page on the AGI Zeitgeist that groups
the robot claims in one place. A new site would not be needed for that.
