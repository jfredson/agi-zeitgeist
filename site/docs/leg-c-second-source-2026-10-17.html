Leg C second source: the wider search, 2026-10-17

Written by the agent on 2026-10-17, in a cloud session on the roadmap step "Leg C second source: find a measure of AI's share of frontier research work that is not a company's report on itself (venue AI-contribution disclosures, researcher surveys), and add it to data/world_measures.csv". It follows the reading note of the same day on the ICLR 2026 AI-disclosure audit (docs/leg-c-second-source-2026-10.md), which found that audit to be context rather than a second source, and it does not go over that ground again.

Result: nothing found qualifies. No row was added to data/world_measures.csv. The scored series, the thresholds file (docs/singularity-entry-thresholds.json) and the definition are untouched. Twenty-six candidates were judged; the three nearest misses, and two questions, are set out for John at the end. This is a reading note, not an amendment.

1. What the step asks for, and the bar each candidate was held to

Leg C of the definition (docs/singularity-entry-definition.md, "AI is doing the improving") is scored from f(t), "the share of inputs to frontier AI progress (code, experiments, research ideas) supplied by AI systems rather than people, from the best measurement available". The definition names three kinds of candidate source: lab self-reports of AI-written code share, surveys of frontier researchers on time saved, and the AI-contribution disclosures that venues require on papers. Today the leg is scored from one of the first kind, Anthropic's R&D Automation Index (John's ruling of 2026-10-17 keeps it scoring), with the company's staff poll and Google's code-share statement beside it unscored.

A candidate qualifies as the second source the step wants when all four hold:

  1. It is a share of the work. A share of code, experiments or research ideas done by AI, or something that can honestly be read as one. A count of papers that declare AI use, a productivity multiplier, or a capability test is not a share of the work.
  2. The work is frontier AI research. The building of the most capable AI systems, not research in general and not software in general.
  3. It is measured by someone other than the company whose work it is. A company's report on its own staff, models or customers is out, whatever its quality; an outside party's re-reading of a company's own figures is still the company's figures.
  4. It is dated, sourced to a primary document, and can be read again. One-off numbers are recorded only as context; a second source has to be something the weekly reading can re-read.

Each candidate below is judged on these four. Where a candidate fails only one, the note says which, because that is what a future reading should watch.

2. How the search was run, and one limit on it

Thirty-eight web searches were run on 2026-10-17 in the order the step sets: venue-level measures first, then researcher surveys, then independent measurements of AI-generated content with a method that separates substance from prose. Every search is logged in data/search_log.csv as leg-c-second-source-002 to leg-c-second-source-039, with reading as the speaker, as the weekly reading's rule for searches that belong to a leg rather than to a speaker.

The limit. The session's network policy refused every direct fetch of a candidate document: the proxy answered 403 for arxiv.org, neurips.cc, blog.neurips.cc, epoch.ai, nature.com and uksg.org (logged as leg-c-second-source-040). Every document below was therefore read through the search tool's extracts of its page, not the page itself. Figures are quoted as the extracts gave them, each with the primary address, and where an extract disagreed with another the disagreement is noted. None of these figures was turned into a row, so nothing in the record rests on this partial reading; but any later session that does add a row from one of these documents must read the page first. The ICLR audit note of the same day was written from the paper itself, in a session that could fetch it.

3.1 Venue-level measures

1. NeurIPS 2026 Position Paper Track, "AI-Generated Papers in the NeurIPS 2026 Position Paper Track" (NeurIPS blog, 2026-06-02, https://blog.neurips.cc/2026/06/02/ai-generated-papers-in-the-neurips-2026-position-paper-track/). The track's chairs ran the Pangram detector (version 3.3.2) over the 969 submissions: a window of text is flagged when the detector's probability exceeds 0.75, and a paper's score is the share of its windows flagged. They report that 28.2% (273 of 969) "substantially used AI for writing", and compare earlier tracks: the 2025 position track at 28.5% of 536 papers and the 2025 datasets and benchmarks track at 5.6% of 996. Secondary reports say 178 papers (18.4%) were desk-rejected on the scores and 123 (12.7%) given conditional notice, and that the rate depends strongly on the window size (42.7% at the default, 12.7% at 100-word windows); those figures were not confirmed against the post. Verdict: no. It measures prose, by a detector whose false-positive rate for this population is disputed, and it says nothing about ideas, experiments or code. Context for how much of the field's writing is machine-written, nothing more.

2. NeurIPS 2025 authors' LLM-use survey. The NeurIPS 2025 LLM policy (https://neurips.cc/Conferences/2025/LLM) does not require declaring LLM use that only supports writing, editing or formatting, and instead asks authors to report such use "in a statistical analysis survey". No published results of that survey were found, on the NeurIPS blog or elsewhere. The one NeurIPS 2025 survey found (datasets and benchmarks track, 851 authors and 155 reviewers) asked about the review process, not about LLM use in the research. Verdict: nothing to read yet. If the results are published they will be the field's own count of declared use by task, worth reading on the same terms as the ICLR audit.

3. ICML 2026. The policy (https://icml.cc/Conferences/2026/Intro-LLM-Policy) allows LLMs "to assist in writing or research" with responsibility on the authors, and publishes no author disclosure statistic. The numbers the conference did publish are about reviewers: hidden watermarks caught 506 reviewers using AI against their declaration, and 497 papers tied to 398 of them were desk-rejected, out of 23,918 submissions. The randomized experiment and survey of LLM use in ICML 2026 peer review (arXiv 2609.19420) is likewise about reviewing. A third-party blog puts authors' self-reported AI use at 9% against 36% detected, with no stated source. Verdict: no. Nothing here is a share of research work.

4. ACL and EMNLP. The Responsible NLP Checklist item E1 asks authors to disclose "the use of AI assistants in research, coding, or writing". The large-scale analysis of EMNLP 2025 checklists (arXiv 2608.09280; 41,607 responses to 23 questions) was found, but the extracts do not break out item E1. The EMNLP 2025 half of the Mohapatra and Pruthi audit (candidate 5) already gives the nearest figure: 40% of accepted papers carry a disclosure statement, and 23.3% of those declare help with code. Verdict: nothing new to read.

5. Mohapatra and Pruthi, "Expectations and Practices around AI Disclosure in CS Research" (arXiv 2608.23271). Read from the paper on 2026-10-17 and judged context, not a second source, in docs/leg-c-second-source-2026-10.md. Unchanged.

3.2 Researcher surveys

6. METR, "Measuring the Self-Reported Impact of Early-2026 AI on Technical Worker Productivity" (2026-05-11, https://metr.org/blog/2026-05-11-ai-usage-survey/). METR, a non-profit that evaluates AI systems and is not an AI company, surveyed 349 technical workers between February and April 2026: 87 software engineers, 71 researchers, 129 academics and PhD students, and 48 founders and managers. Respondents put the median change in the value of their work due to AI at 1.4x to 2x (METR's own announcement says 1.6x to 2.1x "on average"; the two are different summaries of the same data), and the median change in speed at 3x. Asked about the past and future, they put the value multiplier at 1.3x in March 2025, 2x in March 2026 and 2.5x forecast for March 2027. METR flags that self-reports have overstated AI's effect before, that its own staff give the lowest answers of any subgroup, and that its randomized trial of open-source developers found gains of about 4% to 20%. It asks "those interested in tracking the potential automation of AI R&D, such as frontier AI companies" to run surveys building on this one. No question about the share of the respondent's work done by AI was found; how respondents were recruited was not found. Verdict: no, on three of the four tests. A multiplier is not a share and cannot feed the gauge's formula; the sample is technical workers at large, with no frontier-laboratory subgroup reported; and it is one wave with no stated repeat. It is, all the same, the nearest thing found to the definition's "surveys of frontier researchers on time saved" run by someone other than the company, and the first of the three near misses for John.

7. METR, Frontier Risk Report, February to March 2026 (2026-05-19, https://metr.org/blog/2026-05-19-frontier-risk-report/, written with Anthropic, Google, Meta and OpenAI). On AI's part in the companies' own research it reports statements, not figures: Anthropic that "a large percentage of code written at Anthropic is written by AI", Google that AI is used "in almost all work that involves writing code or configuration, technical design, research ideation, to different degrees depending on the task", OpenAI that AI assistance is "embedded in day-to-day R&D workflows". METR's own reading is that agents "probably made researchers more productive" with the size "very uncertain", and that no company relies on agents for setting research agendas, hiring or budgets. Verdict: no. An outside party's report with no number in it; useful as the plainest statement that the companies themselves have not published a share.

8. METR, review of the "Risks from automated R&D" section of Anthropic's February 2026 risk report (2026-05-08, https://metr.org/blog/2026-05-08-rd-section-anthropic-risk-report-feb-2026-review/). An outside review of a company self-report (an internal survey METR says gives "little evidence", on sample size, question granularity and framing). Verdict: no. It produces no figure of its own, but it is the kind of outside audit of a self-report that the earlier note listed as one of three shapes a second source could take, and it says the self-report it audited is weak.

15. Springer Nature, Fudan University and the Shanghai Academy of AI for Science, "AI for Science 2026" (https://www.nature.com/immersive/partnercontent/fudan/aiforscience/index.html) and the white paper "The State of AI Use Among Researchers" drawn from the same survey. Fieldwork 2026-03-02 to 2026-03-30; 10,480 qualified responses from 117 countries and 12 disciplines, weighted by country. It asks how often AI is used for each research activity. Using AI "every time" or "most times": information gathering 43.8%, improving or editing papers 44.6%, planning research 40.9%, reviewing papers 23.5%, deciding where to submit 25.7%. Frequent use falls with seniority. No item on the share of work done by AI, and no breakdown for computer science or AI researchers, was found in the extracts. Verdict: no. All fields, usage frequency rather than share, and the publisher's own survey of its market. The largest researcher survey of 2026, and context for how far AI use in research generally has gone.

16. Wiley, ExplanAItions 2025 to 2026 (https://www.wiley.com/en-us/about-us/ai-resources/ai-study/). 2,430 researchers worldwide; 84% use AI for some aspect of their work (57% a year earlier), 62% for research or publication tasks (45% a year earlier); coding assistants are among the most frequently used tools but the coding-tool subsample is 150 people. Verdict: no, for the same reasons as 15.

17. Frontiers, survey of 1,645 researchers (field note of 2025-12-15, https://www.frontiersin.org/field-notes/2025/12/15/ai_in_peer_review_dec-25). A secondary write-up says about 70% use AI for writing papers and "nearly a quarter" for analysis, design or methodology; the quarter was not confirmed against the publisher's own report. Verdict: no.

18. Google, "AI in Science: Early Insights" (Codreanu and Imas, September 2026, https://ai.google/static/documents/AI-in-Science.pdf). A survey of 637 active researchers in the United States and United Kingdom in July and August 2026, run by a company. Verdict: no. Not frontier AI research, and company-run; noted so that it is not re-found.

19. Nature's reports on researcher surveys. "How are researchers using AI? Survey reveals pros and cons for science" (2025-02-04, https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-025-00343-5) reports the Wiley survey of close to 5,000 researchers in more than 70 countries; Nature's own 2023 survey of 1,600 researchers (https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-023-02980-0) found about 30% using AI in their research. Verdict: no. General adoption, superseded by 15 and 16.

22. AI Impacts, Expert Survey on Progress in AI. The last wave is 2023 (2,778 respondents at six AI venues); no 2025 or 2026 wave was found, and the 2023 instrument has no item on the share of the respondent's work done by AI. Verdict: nothing to read. The one standing survey of AI researchers themselves, and the natural home for such an item if it were ever added.

23. "A survey of generative AI adoption and perceived productivity among scientists who program" (arXiv 2512.19644; 868 scientists recruited through mailing lists). Adoption and perceived productivity; the strongest predictor of perceived productivity is how many lines of generated code the person accepts at once. No share figure found. Verdict: no.

3.3 Independent measurements of AI's contribution to research

9. Epoch AI, "In August, 25% of math preprints acknowledged AI use, up from 4% in April" (data insight, 2026-09-18, https://epoch.ai/data-insights/math-preprints-disclosed-ai-use; data and code at https://github.com/epoch-research/ai-math-arxiv, updated 2026-10-06). Epoch, an independent research group, read every mathematics preprint on arXiv from January 2025 to August 2026: papers containing an AI keyword were classified by a language model (GPT-5.6 Sol, with Claude Fable 5 as a validator) into six kinds of acknowledged use (substantial research, research assistance, writing, code and computation, literature review, formalization), with a verbatim quote kept for each tag; human reviewers agreed with the classification on 93% of 200 acknowledgments. The share of preprints acknowledging any AI use rose from 4% in April 2026 to 25% in August 2026, and 6% credited AI with a "substantial research contribution", defined as "approximately at the level of a coauthor". The rise holds, within 1.5 percentage points each month, when restricted to papers with at least one author who published regularly before 2023. Epoch says the trend "reflects changes in disclosure behavior in addition to changes in AI use". Verdict: no, on two tests. The field is mathematics, not frontier AI research, and the measure is the share of papers that acknowledge a contribution, not the share of the work AI did. But it is the only outside measurement found that separates substantive contribution from prose, has a published method with a human check, open data, a monthly series and an explorer that is being updated. It is the second near miss, and the thing a future reading should most want extended: the same method run on the machine-learning and AI categories of arXiv would be the venue-level measure the step describes, still subject to the "share of work" point.

10. Epoch AI, "Contributions to OpenAI's Codex codebase show signs of AI uplift" (data insight, late July 2026 by the Epoch Brief of 2026-07-31; exact date not confirmed; https://epoch.ai/data-insights/codex-engineer-effort). Epoch took the 7,524 merged pull requests of 41 core contributors to OpenAI's public Codex repository and had an ensemble of three frontier models estimate how long each change would take an experienced engineer without AI. The share of contributor-days with at least 24 hours of estimated unassisted effort rose from about 2% in the second quarter of 2025 to about 8% in the second quarter of 2026 (7.2% in the 24-to-48-hour band and 1.0% above). Epoch calls the figure noncausal and an upper bound on time saved. Verdict: no. It is an uplift proxy on a product codebase, not a share of research work, and Codex is a tool rather than frontier model research. It is the third near miss because of what it shows is possible: an outside party measuring a company's AI-assisted output from public artefacts, with no company figure involved.

11. Epoch AI, "Can AI automate AI R&D yet? Early evidence from InnovationEval: No" (2026-10-07, David Owen, https://epoch.ai/publications/innovationeval). Two frontier agents, 3,000 GPU-hours each, were asked to invent a post-training method matching a recent human one without seeing the paper; the best reached 15% of the human method's gain after out-of-scope changes were removed (about 35% under generous grading), and both overstated their results. Verdict: no. A capability test on one research problem, not a measure of the share of work. Context for leg C in the same way METR's benchmarks are context for leg A.

12. Epoch AI, data insight on OpenAI researchers' coding-agent usage doubling about monthly (2026-10-05). Built on OpenAI's own published figures (candidate 13). Verdict: no. An outside party's reading of a company's numbers is still the company's numbers.

14. Alan Chan, Ranay Padarath, Joe Kwon, Hilary Greaves and Markus Anderljung (GovAI and the University of Oxford), "Measuring AI R&D Automation" (arXiv 2603.03992, March 2026, with later versions to v3). A framework of 14 metrics for the extent of AI R&D automation, among them the capital share of AI R&D spending, researcher time allocation and staff surveys, with the finding that existing evidence is thin and that no single metric suffices; it recommends that companies and third parties such as non-profits start tracking them. Verdict: not a measure. It is the clearest statement of what the step is waiting for, from outside the companies, and it says that as of March 2026 nobody was publishing it.

20. Stanford AI Index 2026, chapter 5, Science (with Schmidt Sciences, https://hai.stanford.edu/assets/files/ai_index_report_2026_chapter_5_science.pdf). Reports AI's share of scientific publications by field (5.8% to 8.8% in 2025, up from under 1% in 2010) and benchmark results on research tasks. No survey of researchers on AI's share of their work was found in the chapter. Verdict: no.

21. International AI Safety Report 2026 (2026-02-03, arXiv 2602.21012). States that "current empirical evidence on AI-assisted research automation is mixed" and gives benchmark results and expert forecasts, no share of work. Verdict: no.

24. Detector studies of AI-written prose. arXiv 2601.17036 estimates LLM-generated content in 21.4% of computer-science review papers and 14.0% of non-review papers on arXiv; a Pangram Labs analysis puts about 21% of ICLR 2026 reviews as fully AI-generated (76,139 reviews, per a third-party report); an earlier study put NeurIPS 2024 at 0.7% of papers and 20.9% of reviews LLM-aided. Verdict: no. Prose and reviews, not research work.

25. Simone Daniotti, Johannes Wachs, Xiangnan Feng and Frank Neffke, "Who is using AI to code?" (Science). A classifier run over more than 30 million GitHub commits finds that 29% of Python functions committed by developers in the United States look AI-written, with data to the end of 2024. Verdict: no. All public software, not research, and not frontier AI; the independent counterpart of the Google code-share row's kind of measure, and context for it.

26. Agents4Science 2025 (https://www.nature.com/articles/s41587-025-02963-8). A conference of AI-authored papers: about 315 submissions, 48 accepted. Verdict: no. A demonstration of what AI authors can get through review, not a share of any field's work.

13. OpenAI, "Research acceleration: the view inside OpenAI" (2026-09-06, https://openai.com/index/research-acceleration-view-inside-openai/). Found on the way. By mid-August 2026 OpenAI's research organization was running 3.1 eight-hour agent-workdays for every human workday, and experiments per active experimenter reached a high in August 2026, which OpenAI links to Codex adoption while noting that compute had also grown. Verdict: excluded by the rule. A company's report on itself, and a runtime ratio rather than a share. It is recorded here because it is the first OpenAI self-report of the same kind as the Google and Anthropic rows, and whether to record it beside the gauge is John's call (section 5).

4. Why the search came up empty, in one paragraph

The three shapes of second source the earlier note listed are all still missing. No one outside a frontier laboratory has surveyed its researchers on the share of their work AI does: METR's survey is of technical workers at large and asks about value, not share. No venue publishes the share of research work declared as AI-done: the venues publish detector rates on prose and counts of declarations, and the one standing audit (Mohapatra and Pruthi) counts declarations. No outside audit of a company's R&D automation figure exists: METR reviewed Anthropic's survey and found it weak, and GovAI's framework says the metrics are not yet collected by anyone. The nearest outside measurements are of the wrong field (mathematics), the wrong unit (a multiplier, a count of acknowledgments, an effort estimate), or the wrong work (a product codebase, public software in general).

5. For John

  1. Whether any near miss should sit beside the gauge as context, unscored. The three are the METR survey (a 1.4x to 2x median value multiplier, February to April 2026), the Epoch mathematics series (6% of August 2026 math preprints crediting AI with a substantial research contribution) and the Epoch Codex measurement (8% of contributor-days at very high estimated effort in the second quarter of 2026). None is a measure of f, so none would be scored, and the earlier note's rule was followed: context rows go in only if John wants them. Two of the three would need a unit the validator does not yet accept for data/world_measures.csv (a multiplier; a share of contributor-days), which is a small validator change, not a method change. The agent's recommendation is to add none of them: a row in that file is read on the page as a measure of the leg, and each of these would need a sentence of explanation to stop it being read that way.
  2. The OpenAI self-report (3.1 agent-workdays per human workday, 2026-09-06). It is excluded from this step by the rule but is the same kind of row as Google's code share. Under the weekly reading's rules it would be flagged, not appended, because it is not a repeat of a series in the file. John can have it recorded as a self-report row with its own speaker, or left out.
  3. Whether the roadmap step is waiting for something that may not come. As of 2026-10-17 the measure the step describes is not published by anyone, and the two outside groups closest to it (METR, GovAI) say so. The step can stay open as a standing watch (section 6), or John can narrow it to the one shape most likely to appear first: Epoch's acknowledgment method run on AI preprints, or a second wave of METR's survey with a frontier-laboratory subgroup.
  4. Nothing here proposes a scored series. A qualifying measure would have been proposed as an amendment for John to rule on; none qualified, so there is no amendment to propose.

6. What a future reading should watch

  • NeurIPS. Results of the 2025 authors' LLM-use survey, if published; the 2026 main-track policy and any disclosure statistics with it; a repeat of the position-track detector audit.
  • ICLR 2027. ICLR's disclosure policy and any statistics the programme chairs publish; a repeat of Mohapatra and Pruthi's audit, which would make the ICLR count a series.
  • Epoch AI. The mathematics series' monthly updates in the explorer, and above all any extension of the same method to the machine-learning and AI categories of arXiv. Also any repeat of the Codex measurement on other public repositories of frontier companies.
  • METR. A second wave of the technical-worker survey, or the company surveys it asked for if a third party runs them; the next Frontier Risk Report's section on R&D automation.
  • GovAI. Any third party adopting the "Measuring AI R&D Automation" metrics.
  • AI Impacts. A 2026 or 2027 wave of the expert survey, and whether it adds an item on the share of the respondent's own work done by AI.
  • Search terms that found the most in this reading: "AI R&D automation" with "measure" or "survey"; "acknowledged AI" with "preprints"; "LLM policy" with the conference name and year; "disclosure statements" with "ICLR" or "NeurIPS". The weekly reading's leg C item may want them; that document is the reading's own and is not changed here.
