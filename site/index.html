Are we in the singularity?

Not yet.

Four things would all have to hold for a year. Today none of them does.

The singularity is the idea that machines will one day get smarter so fast that the world changes faster than anyone can keep up with. This site checks whether that has started.

The current zeitgeist

"Zeitgeist" is a German word for the mood of a time: what people are saying and what they expect. This part of the site records what well-known experts, surveys of researchers, opinion polls, betting markets and online forums have said since 2005 about artificial general intelligence, or AGI for short: a machine that can do most of the thinking work a person can. Every quote carries its date and a link to where it was said.

Has the argument moved: from whether such a machine can be built, to when it will arrive, to whether today's machines are nearly there, or already making themselves smarter? Among the five people traced so far, it has moved, but not all the way. "When will it arrive?" is still what they have talked about most in recent years. "Is today's machine close?" grew from nothing in 2012 to more than half of what they said around 2019, and so far in 2026 it has overtaken "when?" again. "Can it make itself smarter?" first shows up in 2023. And even on the online forum most eager for it, almost no one says it has arrived.

So far this rests on five well-known people (Ray Kurzweil, Geoffrey Hinton, Yann LeCun, Gary Marcus and Sam Altman), three surveys of AI researchers, opinion polls, betting markets and one online forum. More people and sources are being added.

Are we in the singularity?

"Any prediction made now about the next five years will look stupid in retrospect." That line, from a post on an online forum, is one of three classic ways of saying what the singularity is. The other two: machines getting better faster and faster, and machines doing the work of making machines better. Each of the three, on its own, gives false alarms. A fast decade in any industry looks like the first; every new technology makes old forecasts look foolish, so the second trips on the internet too; and a company's claim that its AI does its research cannot be checked from outside. So this test requires all three at once, plus a fourth that is measured by people outside the AI world: whether the economy has changed gear. Each condition has a finishing line, fixed before anyone looked at the numbers, and a bar showing how far it has come toward that line since 2019. The shortest bar is how far we are from the mark. The full rules are in a working document.

Condition A. How long a task the best AI can finish

The measure is how long a job the best AI can finish on its own. METR, a research group that tests AI systems, gives each new system a set of computer-programming jobs of known length, from seconds to days of a skilled person's time, and records the longest it can get right half the time. That measure was chosen because it keeps rising, while most tests of AI get maxed out within a year or two. The upper chart shows each system on the day it came out. The lower chart is the number this condition is about: how long it took that job length to double, measured over the previous twelve months. A flat line would mean steady, fast growth, which is remarkable but not a singularity; a falling line, doubling times getting shorter, is what this condition looks for. The score uses the latest twelve-month window with no reading past 16 hours, because METR says its tests cannot measure beyond that reliably. The condition also requires the doubling time to have fallen two windows in a row, which today it has not.

Condition B. Are forecasts holding up?

The real test would take old predictions of the measure above, compare them with what then happened, and ask how far ahead the predictions still beat the simplest guess, which is that the trend continues. Nobody has built that record yet, and it can only be scored once the predicted dates have passed, so this condition cannot be scored today. What exists now is a stand-in: the year AI researchers expect machines to match people at every task. If the researchers had the trend right, that year would stand still, and the number of years left until it would fall by one every year, the dashed line in the chart. Instead the three surveys show the years left collapsing: between 2022 and 2023, about 14 years of expected waiting disappeared in 1.7 years of real time. That is predictions failing in one direction, too slow, which is the direction this condition records.

Condition C. Is AI doing the work of improving AI?

This condition asks how much of the work of making the best AI better is now done by AI. Nobody outside the AI companies can measure that, so what exists is what the companies say about themselves. Two kinds are recorded. Google says what share of all its new computer code is written by AI; that is a rough stand-in at best, and it is shown but not scored. Anthropic measures what share of its own AI research its AI leads; that is closer to the question but is judged by the AI itself, and it is the figure scored. The two do not measure the same thing and should not be compared.

Condition D. Has the economy changed gear?

The economist Robin Hanson looked at the whole history of the world's economy and found three eras: hunting, farming and industry. In each era the amount the world produces doubled about a hundred times faster than in the era before: every 230,000 years, then every 860 years, then every 15 years. A fourth era would double it in a few years or less. This condition asks whether that has started, using the World Bank's yearly count of everything the world produces. The finishing line, growth of 30 percent in a year, comes from a 2021 report by Tom Davidson for the research funder Open Philanthropy, so the number is not this project's own invention. It is far above anything in the modern record, where the best year since 1961 was 6.6 percent, and well short of Hanson's "days, not years". This is the one condition measured by people outside the AI world, which is why it is in the test. The cost is that the economy reacts years after the technology does, so it will usually be the furthest behind.

What the test still owes

  1. A trial run on the past. Run on the years 1995 to 2019, the test must say "not yet". The internet boom should trip only condition B, and the years when today's AI methods took off should trip only condition A. The measures do not reach back that far yet, so this has not been done. The test was adopted without it, and that gap is stated here so nobody can say it was hidden. If the trial later trips on those years, the finishing lines are wrong and will be changed, with the change written down.
  2. The record of old predictions, for condition B. Predictions of the measure in condition A, starting with the dates researchers gave in a 2016 survey, set against what then happened. Until it exists, condition B cannot be scored.
  3. A second source for condition C that is not a company talking about itself.

Method and data

Everything on this page is worked out from tables anyone can download, and every row in those tables links to where it came from. What people said and expected is kept in one set of tables, and what the world actually did (how good AI got, what the companies reported, how the economy grew) in another; the two are never mixed. Every search that was run to find a quote is written down, so that someone else can run it again. The working documents behind the site are published as they are written, in the project's own terms, for anyone checking the work: the method, the rules for sorting statements, the rules of the test, and every change, with its date and reason. This page is the plain account of the same material.

The searching and the sorting of statements were done by Claude, an AI model made by Anthropic, following those written rules; every rule, and every finishing line of the test, was decided by John Fredrickson. The forum posts were sorted by three AI systems from three companies with no person checking them, and systems from different companies can still share blind spots.
